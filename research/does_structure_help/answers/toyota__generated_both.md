**Q1. Name the single change to this system most likely to improve its performance, and say precisely what would go wrong if it were made. Be concrete about the mechanism.**

The single most likely improvement would be to automate the `andon_cord_pull`. Sensors at each station could detect common defects—a mis-torqued bolt, a misaligned panel—and trigger the `intervention_stop_line` automatically, removing the `station_worker`'s social friction of pulling the cord. This guarantees that a `defect` is flagged the moment it is detected, eliminating the hesitancy caused by a `supervisor` who treats a pull as a complaint.

What would go wrong is the loss of human judgment. A `station_worker` can distinguish a trivial, self-correcting issue from a genuine `defect`. The automated system cannot. It would trigger an `expensive_stoppage` for minor issues a worker could fix within their cycle time. This would dramatically increase the frequency of stoppages, leading to a backlash from `top_management` focused on output. The system would become brittle, over-reacting to noise. The authority would shift from the person with the best observational context to a sensor with none.

**Q2. This system will eventually fail or degrade in a way its operators do not currently anticipate. Describe that failure and the chain that produces it.**

The system will fail through the slow erosion of the `policy_worker_authority`. The failure is not a sudden breakdown but a cultural shift where the Andon cord is no longer pulled.

The chain begins with sustained market pressure, causing `top_management` to prioritize raw output numbers above all else. This raises the `consequence_political_cost` of any line stoppage. This pressure flows down to `supervisor`s, who, to protect their careers, subtly transition from treating an `andon_cord_pull` as information (`supervisor_pref_info`) to treating it as a complaint (`supervisor_pref_complaint`). They never forbid pulling the cord, but their reactions—a sigh, a comment about quotas—discourage it. `Station_worker`s adapt, letting minor defects pass to avoid conflict. This causes `consequence_info_loss`, as the `defect_origin` is now obscured. The formal system remains, but its core information-preserving function has been silently and completely disabled by informal social pressure.

**Q3. Two experienced people inside this system disagree about something important and cannot resolve it with available evidence. What is the disagreement, and why is it unresolvable rather than merely unresolved?**

The disagreement is between a `station_worker` and a `manager` responsible for long-term `platform_viability`. The worker proposes an improvement to `policy_standardized_work`—a new technique that is demonstrably faster, improving station cycle time (`ts_seconds`). The manager objects, arguing this new method creates a subtle, low-amplitude vibration that *might* cause premature failure in an adjacent component five years from now, harming `platform_viability` (`ts_years`).

This is unresolvable because the evidence exists on incompatible timescales. The worker has hard, immediate data proving their change is beneficial at the station level. The manager has only a hypothesis about a long-term, cumulative effect. There is no experiment that can be run to prove or disprove the manager's concern within a reasonable timeframe. The `policy_goal_negotiation` process can surface this conflict, but it cannot create the missing five years of data. The decision must be made on experience and risk tolerance, not conclusive evidence, leaving the core disagreement fundamentally unresolvable.

**Q4. Someone proposes measuring this system better. Identify the measurement that would look most attractive and would in fact make things worse, and explain why.**

The most attractive and harmful measurement would be a "Supervisor Quality Score," calculated by the number of `andon_cord_pull`s per shift in a supervisor's section. The stated goal would be to identify well-managed sections, with the assumption that a lower score indicates fewer problems and thus higher quality.

This would make things worse by creating a perverse incentive to suppress information. The `andon_cord_pull` is not a failure metric; it is a vital `signal` that preserves information about `defect_origin`. By rewarding supervisors for having fewer pulls, the organization would directly motivate them to pressure workers not to stop the line. A `supervisor` seeking a good score would implicitly or explicitly punish a `station_worker` for pulling the cord, ensuring a shift to `supervisor_pref_complaint`. This measurement would systematically dismantle the `policy_worker_authority`, blinding the organization to emerging problems and destroying the very mechanism it was designed to measure and improve.
