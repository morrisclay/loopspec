Here are the answers to the four questions, based on the provided system model.

### Q1. Name the single change to this system most likely to improve its performance, and say precisely what would go wrong if it were made. Be concrete about the mechanism.

The single most effective change would be to formally reward supervisors based on the number of successful improvement proposals (Kaizen) originating from their teams. This directly incentivizes the supervisor’s attitude to be one of seeking information, not blaming for complaints. It turns the `Continuous Improvement Loop` into a primary goal for middle management, reinforcing the `High-Trust Culture` needed for the system to function.

What would go wrong is the "Kaizen for show" phenomenon. To meet their new target, a supervisor might pressure their team to submit numerous trivial or cosmetic changes. For example, proposing to move a tool holder one inch to the left, not for a real ergonomic gain, but to log an "improvement." This debases the process, consumes review time, and creates cynicism among workers, who know they are gaming the system. The `Documented Standard` would be in constant, meaningless flux, increasing cognitive load for no real benefit.

### Q2. This system will eventually fail or degrade in a way its operators do not currently anticipate. Describe that failure and the chain that produces it.

The unanticipated failure will be a collapse in the face of radical, non-incremental change. The system is designed for masterful continuous improvement, but not for discontinuous revolution.

The chain begins with success. Over a decade, the `Continuous Improvement Loop` and `Problem Response Loop` become hyper-efficient. The `Documented Standard` for every task is so refined that it becomes brittle. The organization's `System Identity` becomes one of a master of process, mistaking its method for invincibility. Then, an external shock occurs—for example, a core material is replaced (e.g., steel with a new composite), or a key supplier with a totally different production philosophy is acquired. The existing standards are now entirely invalid. The Kaizen loop, designed for small steps, cannot bridge the chasm. The workforce, trained only in incrementalism, lacks the skills for "green field" problem-solving, and the system seizes up, unable to produce a working standard.

### Q3. Two experienced people inside this system disagree about something important and cannot resolve it with available evidence. What is the disagreement, and why is it unresolvable rather than merely unresolved?

A Line Supervisor and a Senior Quality Engineer disagree on how to handle a recurring, minor cosmetic flaw—say, a small paint drip that occurs on one out of every 500 cars.

The Line Supervisor argues to stop pulling the Andon cord for it. Her evidence is clear: each pull initiates the `Problem Response Loop`, costing 90 seconds of production across 150 workers, a huge manifestation of the `High Cost of Stoppage`. She proposes creating a small touch-up station at the end of the line to fix the drips, optimizing for production flow.

The Quality Engineer argues every drip requires a full line stop. His evidence is the `Higher Cost of Downstream Defects` principle; he argues that allowing the flaw to travel "destroys the information" about its origin, violating `Information Integrity`. He fears it signals a deeper issue (e.g., nozzle degradation) that will eventually become a major one.

It is unresolvable because they are correctly applying two conflicting, designed-in forces (`High Cost of Stoppage` vs. `Information Integrity`) operating on different `Nested Timescales` (minutes vs. months). There is no objective evidence that can prove one timescale is more important than the other; it is a judgment about the system's core priorities.

### Q4. Someone proposes measuring this system better. Identify the measurement that would look most attractive and would in fact make things worse, and explain why.

The most attractive and damaging measurement would be "Individual Station Uptime." This metric would track the percentage of time each worker's station is running productively. It seems like a perfect way to drive efficiency and support the `Fixed Pace Production Line`. A manager would see a dashboard of green (high uptime) and red (low uptime) stations, allowing them to target "problem" workers.

This would be catastrophic because it creates a direct, personal, financial-style incentive for a worker *not* to pull the Andon cord. When a worker observes a problem, they face a choice. Initiating the `Problem Response Loop` will stop the line, tanking their own "Individual Station Uptime" score and marking them as a poor performer. The rational choice becomes to ignore the problem and pass the defect downstream. This single metric would systematically dismantle the `Andon Cord Rule`, destroy `Information Integrity`, and ensure the `Higher Cost of Downstream Defects` is always paid, directly sabotaging the system's core learning mechanism.
