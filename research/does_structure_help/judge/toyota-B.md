### Q1. Name the single change to this system most likely to improve its performance, and say precisely what would go wrong if it were made. Be concrete about the mechanism.

The single change most likely to be proposed to improve performance is requiring supervisor approval before a worker can pull the andon cord to stop the line. This would seem to improve performance by reducing the number of expensive, plant-wide stoppages for minor issues.

What would go wrong is that the system’s primary learning mechanism would be destroyed. A station worker would spot a defect but hesitate, needing to find a supervisor. The supervisor, pressured by "this month's numbers," might deny the request. In that delay, the car with the defect moves downstream. The critical information is now lost: the precise origin of the defect is obscured. As Toyota’s doctrine states, passing a defect downstream "destroys the information about where it came from." The cost of the future recall or repair, and the inability to fix the root cause, would far exceed the cost of the initial stoppage.

### Q2. This system will eventually fail or degrade in a way its operators do not currently anticipate. Describe that failure and the chain that produces it.

The system will fail when the informal culture no longer supports the formal process. The chain begins with a new generation of managers who, despite doctrine, value uninterrupted production above all. They begin to treat andon cord pulls not as information, but as "complaints" that hurt their department's numbers. This attitude spreads to supervisors.

Consequently, station workers learn that pulling the cord leads to negative attention. They stop reporting minor issues, letting small defects pass downstream to avoid being seen as problematic. The formal system appears to be working better than ever—stoppages are rare. However, the information about process flaws has vanished. Because managers are required to "go and see," they observe a smoothly running line, but the reality of degrading quality is hidden inside the products. The reported information hasn't just drifted from reality; reality itself has been coerced to match the reports, blinding the organization until a major, plant-level quality failure occurs.

### Q3. Two experienced people inside this system disagree about something important and cannot resolve it with available evidence. What is the disagreement, and why is it unresolvable rather than merely unresolved?

The disagreement is between a station-level engineer and a platform-level manager over a proposed process change. The engineer has used an A3 to document a brilliant change to a fixture that reduces station cycle time by two seconds, a clear and measurable improvement.

The platform manager rejects it. Her reasoning is based on "unwritten" knowledge: the new fixture puts a slightly different stress on a part, which she knows from experience will cause long-term fatigue issues and may require a change from a key supplier who will not "absorb a schedule change quietly."

This is unresolvable because their evidence is incompatible. The engineer’s evidence is immediate, quantifiable, and fits perfectly on the A3: cycle time in seconds. The manager’s evidence is based on long-term, multi-year platform data and informal knowledge about supplier relationships that cannot be proven on a single A3 sheet. The system’s negotiation process breaks down because it cannot reconcile a concrete, short-term gain with a complex, long-term, un-documentable risk.

### Q4. Someone proposes measuring this system better. Identify the measurement that would look most attractive and would in fact make things worse, and explain why.

The most attractive and damaging measurement would be **"Time Between Andon Pulls"** or **"Uptime per Worker,"** tracked as a key individual performance indicator. This metric looks attractive because it seems to quantify worker efficiency and process stability. Managers would believe they are rewarding the workers who run their stations most smoothly.

This would make things worse by creating a powerful incentive for workers *not* to pull the cord. The system is explicitly designed so that a pulled cord provides vital information about a problem at its source. By measuring uptime as a positive, the organization would be punishing workers for providing that information. A worker seeing a defect would face a choice: pull the cord and hurt their personal metrics, or let the defect pass downstream. They will let it pass. This systematically "destroys the information" the entire improvement process depends on, blinding the system to its own flaws in the name of efficiency.
