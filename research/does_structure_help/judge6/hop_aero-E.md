**Q1. Name the single change to this system most likely to improve its performance, and say precisely what would go wrong if it were made. Be concrete about the mechanism.**

The single most performance-enhancing change would be for the US Air Force to replace Hop Aero's venture funding with a single, five-year, $50M prime contract. This would solve the "clock mismatch" by aligning the company's financial runway with the customer's procurement cycle, removing the existential pressure from YC's quarterly milestones.

What would go wrong is the loss of "learning efficiency." The pressure of the fast VC clock is precisely what forced the founders to invent the cheap, reversible tethered test. With a guaranteed five-year budget, that pressure evaporates. The mechanism for failure is this: the incentive to find low-cost ways to acquire evidence would be replaced by the incentive to spend the contract's budget. The team would likely revert to a traditional, more expensive test sequence, burning through vehicles and time, losing the very capital efficiency and cleverness that made them a promising bet in the first place.

**Q2. This system will eventually fail or degrade in a way its operators do not currently anticipate. Describe that failure and the chain that produces it.**

The unanticipated failure will be "success-induced requirement creep." The chain begins when Hop Aero executes a successful full-profile test far ahead of schedule. This success elevates the project's visibility within the Air Force, attracting attention from stakeholders outside the original programme office. These new, higher-ranking parties, seeing a proven capability, will begin adding requirements: a different sensor payload, compatibility with a different C2 system, or a 20% increase in throw-weight.

The "dial" is no longer held by one customer but becomes a committee. Hop Aero, dependent on the Air Force for its future, is forced to chase these moving goalposts. The clean, focused design of the Rook rocket becomes compromised by these late-stage additions, leading to cascading delays and technical debt. The system degrades not from a launch failure, but from the political weight of its own success, becoming an over-specified and underperforming compromise.

**Q3. Two experienced people inside this system disagree about something important and cannot resolve it with available evidence. What is the disagreement, and why is it unresolvable rather than merely unresolved?**

The disagreement is between the lead propulsion engineer and the lead guidance engineer over the objective of the first low-altitude, free-flight test. The propulsion lead insists the flight must test a high-thrust, steep ascent profile to validate the engine's performance under maximum dynamic pressure. The guidance lead argues the priority must be a less aggressive launch followed by a high-G mid-course correction to validate the guidance system's stability through the transonic phase.

This is unresolvable because the evidence needed to determine which subsystem carries more risk can only be acquired by flying the test. The flight profiles are mutually exclusive, and as the text states, "finding out costs a vehicle." They have only one prototype rocket for this flight. There is no existing data, simulation, or ground test that can definitively prove whether the engine or the guidance system is more likely to fail. The disagreement is a bet on which unknown is more dangerous, and the cost of resolving it is the very vehicle they are arguing about.

**Q4. Someone proposes measuring this system better. Identify the measurement that would look most attractive and would in fact make things worse, and explain why.**

The most attractive and harmful measurement would be "Weekly Milestone Velocity"—a burn-down chart tracking the number of discrete engineering sub-tasks completed. This would be incredibly attractive to YC and other investors, as it translates the messy, uncertain process of hardware development into a familiar, software-style metric of progress, satisfying their "fast clock."

This would make things worse by incentivizing the team to optimize for activity, not for knowledge. The team would prioritize breaking work into small, easily-cleared tasks (e.g., "verify capacitor spec," "run simulation #247") that inflate the metric but do not address the core, integrated risks. It would actively discourage them from designing clever, system-level experiments like the tethered test, because such a project is slow to complete and represents only a single "milestone." They would sacrifice genuine, expensive learning for the appearance of predictable, rapid progress, steering them directly away from solving the central problem that "finding out costs a vehicle."
