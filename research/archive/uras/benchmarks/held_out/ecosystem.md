# The North Sea Cod Fishery: Setting the TAC

The whole year for the North Sea cod fishery pivots around the December meeting of the EU-UK Specialised Committee on Fisheries. But the work starts long before. In the spring and early autumn, research vessels chartered by ICES—the International Council for the Exploration of the Sea—steam out to conduct their groundfish surveys. They tow a standard survey trawl at predetermined locations, a grid laid over the whole North Sea. They haul the gear, and the scientists on board count, weigh, and measure every cod that comes up. They take otoliths—ear bones—to determine the age of the fish.

This survey data is combined with commercial data from the previous year. Every skipper has to keep a logbook detailing what they caught, where, and how long they towed for. When they land their catch in a port like Peterhead or Hanstholm, samples are often taken by the national authority—the Marine Management Organisation in the UK, for instance. All this information—the survey catches and the commercial landings data—is fed into stock assessment models. These are complex statistical constructions, Virtual Population Analyses, that try to work backwards from the fish that were caught to figure out how many must have been in the sea to begin with.

The key outputs are the Spawning Stock Biomass (SSB), an estimate of the total weight of mature fish capable of reproducing, and Fishing Mortality (F), a measure of the proportion of the stock killed by fishing. Based on these numbers, and a target to achieve Maximum Sustainable Yield (MSY), the ICES scientists issue their advice in late autumn: a figure for the Total Allowable Catch (TAC) for the following year.

This is where the science ends and the politics begins. The scientific advice is just that—advice. The national representatives go into the December meeting armed with this number, but also with intense pressure from their own fishing industries. A skipper with a £2 million boat and a mortgage to pay sees a drastic TAC cut as a direct threat to his livelihood. The processors on shore need a steady supply of fish to keep their plants running and their workers employed. The politicians know that coastal communities can be devastated by a poor fishing year. So, they haggle. They trade paper fish. A bit more cod quota might be traded for a bit less haddock. The final TAC agreed upon is almost always a compromise, often set higher than the initial scientific advice. That number is then divided up into national quotas, which are then allocated to individual Producer Organisations or directly to vessels. From January 1st, the skippers go out to try and catch their share.

## What people are trying to judge that they cannot see directly

The single biggest unknown is the true size of the stock. The SSB is an estimate from a model, based on a survey that samples a tiny fraction of the sea. Every skipper has a story about how the survey missed the fish that year, or how the scientists were surveying in the wrong place at the wrong time. They are trying to guess at the *real* number of fish under the water, not the number on the scientist's spreadsheet.

The other crucial ghost is recruitment. How many young cod from the last spawning season have survived and will grow big enough to enter the fishery in the next year or two? This is hugely variable and depends on sea temperature, plankton availability, and predation. The models have a stock-recruitment function, but it's a statistical guess at best. A skipper might see the grounds covered in "pinger" codling too small to keep, and know a good year-class is on its way, long before it shows up in the official assessment.

## What makes it hard

The time lags are killer. The assessment for next year's quota is based on last year's commercial data and this year's survey. The decision is made in December, and the fishing happens all through the following year. You're always driving by looking in the rearview mirror.

It's a mixed fishery. Nobody just catches cod. When you put a trawl in the water, you get haddock, whiting, plaice, and a dozen other things. The big problem is "choke species." If the cod TAC is cut drastically but the haddock TAC is high, a boat might catch its entire annual cod quota as bycatch while it's still trying to catch haddock. Under the landing obligation (the "discard ban"), they can't throw the cod back. Once their cod quota is gone, they're tied to the dock, even if they have plenty of haddock quota left. This creates an enormous economic incentive to find ways around the rules—to misreport catches or illegally discard.

Finally, the environment isn't standing still. The North Sea is warming. Cod are a cold-water species, and their centre of abundance is slowly shifting north. The old fishing grounds aren't as productive as they used to be, and the historical data the models rely on is becoming a less reliable guide to the future.

## Who bears the cost when it goes wrong

If the TAC is set too low based on a pessimistic assessment, the cost is immediate and borne entirely by the fishing industry and its dependent communities. Skippers can't make their payments, deckhands are laid off, and the towns that depend on the landings suffer. The cost is economic and social.

If the TAC is set too high, year after year, the cost is ecological and delayed, but ultimately much greater. The Spawning Stock Biomass gets fished down below the point where it can reliably replenish itself. The stock collapses. This happened to North Sea cod in the early 2000s. When that happens, the fishery is closed or reduced to almost nothing for years. The boats go bankrupt anyway, the communities wither, and a natural resource that should have been available for generations is gone. The long-term cost is borne by everyone, including future generations who can no longer fish for North Sea cod.

## What everyone knows but nobody writes down

Everyone knows that a skipper's skill is a huge variable. Two identical boats with the same quota can have wildly different results. A good skipper knows how to read the tide, the seabed on the plotter, and the marks on the sounder to know exactly where the fish are holding. This 'tacit knowledge' is worth more than any scientific model, but it can't be quantified.

Everyone knows that "black fish"—unreported or misreported landings—happens. The pressure from choke species makes it almost inevitable. It’s not that people are inherently dishonest; it's that the system sometimes creates impossible choices between following the rule and staying in business.

Everyone knows that the scientific advice is the opening bid in a political negotiation, not the final word. A "strong" fisheries minister is one who comes back from the December meeting having secured a quota higher than the scientists recommended.

And everyone knows that the fish move. A skipper won't waste fuel steaming to a spot that was good last year if he's hearing on the radio that the fish are showing up 50 miles north because of a change in water temperature. The fleet's movement is far more dynamic and responsive than the static maps in the assessment documents.
